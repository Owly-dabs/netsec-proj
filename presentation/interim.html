50.020 Network Security · Interim presentation

Detecting attacks on a water treatment plant from its sensor data

We reproduce two published unsupervised detectors, USAD and GDN, on the SWaT 2015 dataset. We then evaluate them more strictly than the original papers, add plant-aware alarm logic, and test whether the setup transfers to the WADI water distribution testbed.

1 · Problem

Brief and threat model

Course brief: design, implement, and demonstrate or evaluate an attack or anomaly detection system using AI/ML. Reproduce detection mechanisms from academic papers. As a stretch goal, introduce original modifications that make detection more effective or better suited to the use case.

Threat model: an attacker with access to the plant's control network spoofs sensor readings or forces actuators, for example opening a valve to overflow a tank while the HMI shows a normal level. The attacks in SWaT 2015 are of this kind. They are grouped by whether they hit one or several stages, at one or several points.

Detection paradigm: semi-supervised. Models train on normal operation only and raise an alarm when an anomaly score crosses a threshold. Attack labels are used only to evaluate.

2 · Dataset choice

Why SWaT, with WADI as the transfer target

We surveyed the iTrust datasets offered by the course and read the dataset papers for the two options that looked most novel.

EPIC rejected

All eight released scenarios are normal operating configurations, such as generator synchronisation and different loads. The dataset paper (Ahmed & Kandasamy, 2021) has a section titled "Lack of Attack Scenarios on the Process". With no attack data, any evaluation would rely on attacks we inject ourselves.

IoT honeypot rejected

81.5M Zeek logs from 40 VPN-forwarded honeypots (Aung et al., ICSS 2020). Nearly all traffic is hostile and there is no benign class. The authors describe it as unlabelled, and the only ground truth is Suricata alerts. 91% of sessions are SSH brute force against the forwarding VMs, not the IoT devices.

SWaT + WADI chosen

Both datasets come from real testbeds, have labelled attack periods, and are reported in nearly every major time-series anomaly detection paper. That gives us published numbers to reproduce against.

Why GDN is the main reproduction target

  1. A checkable published target. GDN reports SWaT P 99.35 / R 68.12 / F1 0.81 under point-wise scoring, without point-adjust. Kim et al. (AAAI 2022) show that point-adjust inflates F1 until random scores look competitive, and many SWaT results are reported only that way. GDN's numbers are a fair target to check a reproduction against.
  2. Built for a coupled plant. SWaT's six stages are tightly coupled: opening a valve changes a tank level downstream. GDN learns a graph of which sensors depend on each other from sensor embeddings, and forecasts each sensor from its neighbours with graph attention. An attack that breaks an expected dependency, such as a spoofed level that no longer matches the flows around it, shows up as forecast error. USAD, our second target, reconstructs each window as a flat vector, so the two reproductions contrast a graph-based and a reconstruction-based approach.
  3. Names the deviating sensor. GDN's score is the largest normalised per-sensor forecast error, so every alarm points to a sensor. Our stage-wise alarm logic groups these errors by stage (P1–P6) so operators see which stage alarmed. Detectors that output one score per time step, such as USAD, PCA or Isolation Forest, cannot support this.
  4. Feasible on our hardware. We train on CPU only with 14 GB RAM. GDN is small (window 5, embedding 64, top-15 neighbours), and we implement its graph attention in plain PyTorch without torch_geometric. We follow GDN's own preprocessing (10× downsampling, min–max scaling, last 10% of training data for validation), which keeps our numbers comparable and cuts SWaT to about 50k training windows.

Network-based and fusion detection on SWaT

We checked whether detection from network traffic would be the more novel route. A short literature search turned up a small, preliminary body of work:

  • Berge & Li, NIKT 2024 (arXiv 2410.19717) fused Zeek conn.log and cip.log with process data from SWaT A6 (2019) and trained supervised classifiers. Combining sources improved recall. Results are shown only as histograms, and the authors call the work a proof of concept.
  • Mieden & Beltman, OS3 2020 trained neural networks on the 2015 network CSVs and dropped the packet payload. The best F1 was about 0.73, on single-stage single-point attacks only. Other attack types scored about 0.01–0.15.
  • arXiv 2205.08953 (master's thesis) learned representations of raw industrial traffic with a convolutional GRU autoencoder. It did not improve network intrusion detection.

The SWaT attacks change the values inside packets, not traffic volume or timing, so flow features show little. We therefore detect from process data, and leave network fusion as an optional stretch goal.

4 · System design

Pipeline

Preprocessing protocol

We follow GDN's protocol so that our numbers can be compared with the published ones.

  1. Strip whitespace from column names and drop timestamp columns.
  2. WADI only: drop columns that are all NaN or constant in train, forward-fill and then zero-fill gaps, and map label -1 to attack.
  3. Downsample by 10: the median of each 10-row block. A block is labelled an attack if at least half its rows are attacks.
  4. Drop the first 2,160 downsampled training rows (6 h of start-up transient).
  5. Min–max scale with training min and max. Test values are not clipped, because values out of range are themselves signal.
  6. Hold out the last 10% of training rows, in time order, as validation.

6 · Evaluation protocol

Three thresholds, five kinds of metric

Many papers report F1 at the best possible threshold and after point-adjust. Point-adjust counts a whole attack as detected if a single step inside it is flagged. We report that number for comparison, next to scores an operator could actually get.

7 · Original modifications

Stretch goals, in priority order

  1. Rigorous re-evaluation. The matrix above shows how far published-style numbers fall when the threshold must be chosen without labels and recall is counted per step or per event.
  2. Plant-aware alarm logic. GDN gives an error per sensor. We group sensors by stage (P1–P6) and set a validation threshold per stage. We then require k of the last n steps to alarm before raising one. We measure the change in false alarms and in time to detect. The operator sees which stage raised the alarm.
  3. Transfer to WADI. Apply the configuration tuned on SWaT to WADI without re-tuning, to test how well it generalises to a larger, messier plant.
  4. Network and process fusion (optional). Only if we obtain network CSVs for a few attack windows. The question is whether network features catch attacks that the process model misses, or catch them sooner.

11 · Glossary

Terms used on this page

Plant and data

SWaT
Secure Water Treatment: iTrust's six-stage water treatment testbed at SUTD. Its December 2015 dataset is our main benchmark.
WADI
Water Distribution: iTrust's distribution-network testbed. Our transfer target.
EPIC
Electric Power and Intelligent Control: iTrust's power testbed. Rejected because its released data has no attacks on the process.
P1–P6
SWaT's six process stages. The first digit of a tag gives its stage, so LIT301 is in P3.
Sensor / actuator
A sensor measures the process, such as a level or flow. An actuator changes it, such as a valve or pump.
Tag prefixes
FIT flow, LIT level, AIT analyser, PIT / DPIT (differential) pressure sensors; MV motorised valve, P pump, UV ultraviolet dechlorinator.
HMI
Human–machine interface: the operator's screen. A spoofed sensor makes it show false values.
SSSP · SSMP · MSSP · MSMP
SWaT attack categories: single- or multi-stage, at a single point or at multiple points.
Zeek · Suricata · CIP
Zeek turns network traffic into logs such as conn.log. Suricata is a rule-based intrusion detection system. CIP (Common Industrial Protocol) is the industrial protocol recorded in cip.log.
Parquet
Columnar file format we convert the raw xlsx and csv files into for fast loading.
Downsampling ÷10
Replace each block of 10 one-second rows with its median, as GDN does.
Min–max scaling
Rescale each signal to [0, 1] using the training minimum and maximum.
Validation split
The last 10% of normal training data, held out so thresholds can be set without attack labels.

Models

Semi-supervised
Train on normal data only. Attack labels are used only to evaluate.
Anomaly score
One number per time step. Higher means less like normal operation.
PCA
Principal component analysis: projects data onto its main directions of variance. The score is the reconstruction error.
Isolation Forest (iForest)
An ensemble of random splitting trees. Points that are isolated in few splits are anomalous.
Autoencoder
A network that compresses its input and reconstructs it. Poor reconstruction signals an anomaly.
USAD
UnSupervised Anomaly Detection (Audibert et al. 2020): two autoencoders sharing one encoder, trained adversarially.
GDN
Graph Deviation Network (Deng & Hooi 2021): learns a graph of sensor dependencies and forecasts each sensor from its neighbours.
Embedding · top-k
GDN learns a vector per sensor. Each sensor's k most similar sensors become its graph neighbours.
Graph attention
Weights each neighbour's contribution to a forecast by a learned relevance.
Window
The last few time steps fed to a model: 5 for GDN, 12 for USAD.
Robust normalisation
Scale each sensor's error by its median and IQR (interquartile range), which outliers barely move.
LSTM · GRU · CUSUM
LSTM and GRU are recurrent neural networks for sequences. CUSUM is a cumulative-sum change detector.
Seed
Random-number seed. We run seeds 0, 1 and 2 and report the mean ± std.

Evaluation and alarms

Threshold
The score value above which a time step is flagged as an alarm.
Precision · recall · F1
The share of alarms that are real attacks, the share of attack steps caught, and their harmonic mean.
Point-wise
Every time step is counted separately as a true positive, false positive or miss.
Point-adjust (PA)
If any step in an attack is flagged, every step of that attack counts as detected. Inflates F1 (Kim et al. 2022).
Event-level
Each attack is counted once, as detected if any of its steps is flagged.
Composite F1
Harmonic mean of point-wise precision and event-level recall (Garg et al. 2021).
Best-F1 oracle
A threshold picked with test labels to maximise F1. Not deployable; reported for comparison with papers.
val_max
GDN's rule: the threshold is the highest score seen on validation data.
val_q99.9
The threshold is the 99.9th percentile of validation scores.
Time to detect (TTD)
Seconds from the start of an attack to the first alarm.
ROC-AUC · PR-AUC
Threshold-free area under the ROC curve and under the precision–recall curve.
Stage-wise alarm
A separate threshold per stage P1–P6, applied to that stage's sensor errors.
k-of-n persistence
Raise an alarm only if k of the last n steps exceed the threshold. We use 3 of 5.
Transfer
Apply the configuration tuned on SWaT to WADI without re-tuning.
Network fusion
Combining network traffic features with process data.
